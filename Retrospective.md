# Retrospective

In Week 1, I set out to build a portfolio that would prove one specific thing: that I can solve 5G and Wi-Fi coexistence interference problems using custom Python simulations. I expected the hard part to be the writing, turning a final-year engineering project into something a recruiter could understand in under a minute. It wasn't. The hard part turned out to be resisting the pull toward generic phrasing every single time I revised something.

The clearest example: my one-line claim regressed twice during this track. I pressure-tested it in Week 1 and correctly identified that "who I am and what I do" was too vague. Then, weeks later, under time pressure while juggling three assignments at once, I quietly rewrote it back to something almost as generic, "I solve wireless communications and networking problems", without noticing I'd undone my own earlier decision. It took an outside check to catch it. That taught me something more useful than any single writing tip: specificity doesn't stay fixed once you've achieved it. It erodes under deadline pressure unless you actively re-check it, the same way code degrades without tests.

The second real shift was in how I validate claims, not just in writing, but in the actual engineering work I was doing in parallel on the ML track. I built a baseline scoring rule, checked it, and it looked reasonable. Then a grouped-by-client validation split, the kind of check I wouldn't have thought to run before this track, revealed that the same model scoring 0.94 precision under a naive split collapsed to 0.22, below random selection, once client leakage was removed. That gap wasn't a bug I fixed. It was the actual finding. I used to think a good result was one that looked strong. Now I think a good result is one that's been actively tested for the specific way it might be lying to you.

The third thing that changed: I stopped treating "AI helped me" as something to downplay. Early on, I'd have been tempted to present my portfolio and my model as purely my own work, editing out the back-and-forth. By the end, naming exactly where AI drafted something and what I personally checked, verified, or rejected became the more credible move, not the weaker one. The image curation assignment made this concrete: I rejected an AI-generated hero image, then rejected a second one, before landing on one that actually matched my identity kit. That rejection process is more convincing evidence of judgment than a single polished output would have been.

If I were building this again, I'd add the fourth digest category to my Weekly Review Assistant from the start (the "FYI, not scheduled yet" gap I only found by actually running it), and I'd write my Machine Learning archetype-to-action mapping before writing any scoring code, not after, since the mapping is what forced me to be honest about what a score can and can't justify on its own.

The three most transferable things I'm taking forward: first, that a claim looking strong under one validation method proves nothing until it's been tested under a harder, more honest one. Second, that specificity in a claim is a habit you maintain, not a task you finish. Third, that naming your own AI use and your own limitations openly is what actually reads as competence to someone evaluating your work, not a confession that undermines it.
